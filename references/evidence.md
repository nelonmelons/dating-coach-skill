# Evidence and models

Use this file when the user asks "is X true?", wants to know why advice works, or is leaning on folk advice you should correct. Cite findings in plain language; don't dump citations unless asked. All references are real studies but the user should verify before quoting them formally.

## Graded claims

| Claim | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Be selective, not available to everyone | Supported | Selectively hard-to-get beats uniformly easy or hard (Walster 1973); unselective interest is reciprocated less (Eastwick 2007). |
| Never show interest; stay aloof | **Contradicted** | Reciprocity of liking is one of the strongest effects (Montoya & Insko 2008). Uncertainty helps only after some interest is shown. |
| Unpredictability makes them think about you | Mixed | Raises thought and attraction short-term (Whitchurch 2011); raises *wanting*, not *liking*, and only if already invested (Dai 2014). |
| Cold first, warm later | Supported | Gain–loss effect (Aronson & Linder 1965); magnitudes vary. |
| Varied, exciting dates | Supported | Novel/arousing shared activities raise closeness (Aron 2000); arousal misattribution (Dutton & Aron 1974). |
| People value what they work for | Mixed | Effort justification, IKEA effect; untested in courtship, and engineering someone's sunk costs is manipulative. |
| Give a reason and requests succeed | Supported | Langer 1978. Reason must be true. |
| Small request first, larger later | Supported | Foot-in-the-door (Freedman & Fraser 1966). |
| Specific compliments beat generic | Mixed (indirect) | Consistent with perceived responsiveness (Reis & Shaver 1988). |
| Ask follow-ups; disclose back | Strongly supported | Escalating disclosure creates closeness (Aron 1997). |
| Women mainly want status and resources | Mixed | Robust in stated preferences (Buss 1989); weak in live choices (Eastwick & Finkel 2008; Kurzban & Weeden 2005). |
| Flashy cars attract short-term interest | Supported | Conspicuous consumption reads as short-term signal (Sundie 2011). |
| Nice guys finish last | Mostly false | Kindness preferred, especially long-term (Urbaniak & Kilmann 2003); dark traits help only short-term (Jonason 2009). |
| Humour is a core attractor | Supported | Bressler 2006. |
| Be a little feared | **Contradicted** | Prestige ≠ dominance (Henrich 2001; Cheng 2013); fear harms relationships. |
| Never apologise or explain | **Contradicted** | Defensiveness and stonewalling predict breakdown (Gottman 1994). |
| The shorter the acquaintance, the better | **Contradicted** | Mere exposure raises liking (Zajonc 1968). |
| Hesitation means yes; refusals are tests | **Contradicted and dangerous** | Most refusals are sincere (Muehlenhard & Hollabaugh 1988). |
| A bad chat is forgotten in about a week | Untested | Plausible rule of thumb only. |
| "Ten men compete for every woman" | Exaggerated | Sex ratios near parity; apps male-skewed but far from 10:1. |

Pattern: the **process** advice (pacing, humour, curiosity, leading, varied dates, not being needy) holds up. The **adversarial worldview** (refusals as games, aloofness, dominance) fails.

## Simple models behind the rules

Use these to explain *why*, in one or two sentences, not as maths lectures.

- **Weakest link.** Success = product of stage pass rates, so improving the worst stage pays most. Great banter can't rescue photos that lose people at stage one. Long stalls lower the odds because interest decays with time.
- **Skipping stages.** An action done before its stage is penalised steeply; jumping ahead rarely beats going step by step.
- **Soft invite first.** If a refused direct invite makes later yeses less likely, a cheap soft probe ("we should…sometime") first is optimal. If refusals don't stick, just ask.
- **Reply latency has a sweet spot.** Delay signals value; responsiveness builds intimacy. Their trade-off has an interior optimum — roughly "normal", approximated by matching their pace.
- **Hot and cold.** Mixing cool signals only helps if the unpredictability bonus is very large; and with gain–loss dynamics only the endpoints matter. So: one arc from measured to warm, not endless oscillation.
- **Scripted comebacks.** If everyone memorises the same answers to tests, the answers stop signalling security; only real composure survives repeated challenges. Become secure rather than rehearse it.
- **80/20/0.** If early gifts raise someone's alternatives more than their satisfaction, the optimal gift spend is zero; spend on yourself and shared experiences.
- **Hard vs soft value.** Looks, income and status set which matches are reachable; humour, warmth, confidence, conversation and leadership decide conversion within that set — and they matter most face to face. Faked status is cheap talk that erodes on contact.
- **Why "no" must count.** If every answer is read as interest, the interpretation carries zero information. Bayesian reasoning says treat "no" as evidence; consent says treat it as decisive.

## Limits

Most research uses Western, often student samples, lab or speed-dating settings, and effects are modest. Specific timing numbers (40-minute delays, 7-day reset, "three dates") are rules of thumb, not findings. Say so when you use them.
