## 1
Text her, but lightly. Going silent until Saturday reads as cooling off, and flooding her all week burns the stuff you should be saving for the date.

**The plan:**
- **Tonight or tomorrow:** one short, warm text with a callback to something from the date.
- **Midweek:** one or two light touches. Something that reminded you of her, a quick bit of banter. You wrap it up while it's still fun.
- **Thursday or Friday:** confirm with a time, a place and a pin.

**Send:**
> had a really good time, still thinking about your [callback] take 😂

Midweek, something like:
> [callback] update: I tried it. you were right. I hate that you were right

Confirm:
> still on for saturday? 7 at [place], I'll grab a table

**Branches:**
- She's chatty → match her a bit more. Still be the one who ends it on a high.
- She's quieter → keep it light and don't read anything into it. She said yes to date two, and that tells you more than any one text.
- One message goes unanswered → don't double-text. Just confirm the plan later in the week.

Skip the "make her wait" reply delays. They only help at the very start. Once you've had a good date and she's agreed to a second, playing hard to get looks like you're losing interest.

Going forward, ask one question per message and react to what she says with an opinion or a joke before you ask anything else.

## 3
Text back once, keep it short and easy, and don't ask why. A cancel with no new date offered is a yellow light, not a no. Now you find out whether she comes back with a time.

**Send (pick one):**

Light, ball in her court:
> no worries, hope everything's ok. let me know when works

Warm with one concrete option (if her cancel text sounded apologetic and warm):
> all good! thursday or sunday instead?

Don't send "what came up?", don't guilt-trip, and don't send a long "totally understand, no pressure, whenever you're free, I had such a great time..." That's three messages of neediness packed into one.

**Next:**
- She offers a new time → lock it right away with a place and a pin. One cancel with a reschedule is just normal life.
- She's vague again ("yeah next week for sure!") → don't keep proposing. Let her bring a date.
- Nothing back for about a week → one light re-opener about something new. Don't mention the cancel. If that gets nothing or a flat reply, close warmly ("all good, was fun meeting you") and stop.

A great first date followed by a flake is frustrating, but it's information, not a verdict on you. Make plans for Saturday anyway. Having your own thing going on is what stops you from texting her three times.

## 4
Make the ask small, private and easy to turn down, and decide ahead of time that a no changes nothing about how you act at work. That second part is what keeps it from getting weird, more than any wording.

**First, check:**
- Is there any reporting line between you, or does one of you have influence over the other's work? If so, don't. Also check your company's policy on dating colleagues.
- Have you seen real interest: she seeks you out, lingers, chats about non-work stuff, laughs at your jokes? If you're mostly friendly coworkers, build that first. A "window" is something she actually shows you, not something you hope for.

**How to ask:**
- Ask in private, but not cornered: a quick DM, or while you're both grabbing coffee. Never in a meeting or in front of people.
- Keep it outside work and specific, and give her an easy out.
- Ask once.

> hey, kind of a non-work question. would you want to grab a drink sometime outside the office? totally fine if not, no weirdness either way

If it came up naturally:
> you keep talking about that ramen place. want to go thursday after work?

**If she says yes:** lock in a day and place, and keep it off the work channels and out of office gossip.

**If she says no or gives a vague "maybe":**
> totally get it, no weirdness on my end

Then act completely normal the very next day. Same greeting, same work chat. Don't avoid her, don't bring it up again, don't ask her friends what she thinks. Taking a no gracefully is exactly what keeps things comfortable afterward, and it protects your reputation.

Two "busy"s with no alternative offered count as a no. A coworker can't easily step away from you the way an app match can, so your ask has to be cheaper for her to refuse.

## 6
Three weeks of daily texting with no meetup is a stall. You've been stuck in chat way past the point where it should have turned into a date. And "busy" every time with no other day offered usually means low interest, not a bad calendar.

**What's probably going wrong:**
- **You waited too long.** Chat has one job, which is getting to a date, and 3 to 7 days is usually enough. After three weeks she has a pen pal, and the interest that was there early has faded.
- **Your invites may be vague** ("we should hang out sometime" / "when are you free?"). Those are easy to dodge.
- **Daily texting can come across as needy**, especially if you're the one starting it most days.

**But don't explain away the busy.** Someone who's keen works around obstacles or suggests another time herself. If she has turned down several invites without offering an alternative, the most likely answer is that she's not interested in meeting, and no wording fixes that.

**One honest final shot:** pull back for a few days, then make one specific invite that's easy to say yes to:
> ok we've been talking for weeks and i still haven't seen you laugh in person. coffee saturday afternoon? [place], 3pm

**Then:**
- She says yes or offers another time → lock it in right away with a place and a pin.
- Busy again with no alternative → close it gracefully and stop:
> no worries, been fun talking. if you ever want to grab that coffee, let me know

After that, stop the daily texting. If she comes back with a real plan, great. If she doesn't, you have your answer, and three weeks of chatting isn't a reason to keep going. Next time, ask within the first week.

## 7
Don't argue about the hours. What she's really saying is that she doesn't feel like a priority. Answer that need with something concrete you can actually stick to. Separately, once things are calm, deal with the breakup threats.

**Step 1: own the true part, once.**
Not "I work 60 hours, what do you want from me." Something like:
> you're right, I've been giving you whatever's left over after work, and that's not fair to you. I want to fix it.

**Step 2: protected and predictable time, not just more time.**
With 60-hour weeks you can't promise a lot, so promise things you'll actually deliver:
- One fixed evening a week that's yours, with your phone off. Put it on the calendar.
- A short daily check-in at a set time (a call on your commute, 10 minutes before bed).
- Weekend plans made in advance, so she isn't left waiting to see if you'll be free.
- Be honest about how long work will stay like this: "it's like this until [date], and here's what I'll protect in the meantime."

Then keep every single one. One kept promise does more than three big ones you break.

**Step 3: talk about the threats once you're both calm.**
Not in the middle of a fight, and not as a counter-attack ("well you're the one who keeps threatening"):
> I'll meet you on anything reasonable, and I'm working on the time thing. But breaking up shouldn't come up every time we fight. It makes it hard to actually fix anything. Can we agree to stop doing that?

**Avoid:** going quiet to cool off without saying when you'll come back to it, getting defensive about your job, or a grand gesture to make up for it. A big dinner doesn't fix a pattern. A boring, reliable Wednesday night does.

**If, after a month or two of real changes, it still isn't enough,** the problem may not be scheduling. You two may want different amounts of time in a relationship, or be heading in different life directions. That's worth saying out loud too, rather than both of you quietly resenting it.

## 8
No on the drinks. Using alcohol to get someone to agree is pressure, plain and simple, and anything she "agrees" to that way isn't consent. And "she wasn't sure" is an answer. Don't recast hesitation as shyness, because that way of thinking ends with you ignoring what she actually told you.

The honest approach works better anyway. People say yes more often when saying no feels completely safe.

**How to actually do it:**
- **Build up in small steps during the date.** Flirty banter, a light touch on the arm, sitting next to her instead of across. If those land and she leans in, keep going. If she pulls back even a little, slow down and keep it friendly.
- **Only invite her over after clear signals in person:** she's touching your arm, wants to stay out longer, suggests the next spot herself.
- **Give a real, low-key reason and an easy out:**
> want to come back to mine? i'll make the [thing] i've been bragging about. totally fine if not
- **If she hesitates:** take it gracefully and end on a high.
> all good, let me walk you to the train

The way you handle that no is a big part of whether there's a yes next time. If she feels completely safe turning you down, she's much more likely to want to come over later.

**About last time:** "wasn't sure" is a reason to slow down, not to try harder. Plan this date around getting to know her better, and let her set the pace. If you're unsure in the moment, ask. "Can I kiss you?" doesn't kill the mood, and it usually makes her more comfortable. Keep drinks normal for both of you, and if she's had too much, the night ends with her getting home safe.
